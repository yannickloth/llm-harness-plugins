# Reader-Guidance Phrases

Reference for the "guide the reader through the artifact" target in both
`natural-prose-auditor` and `natural-prose-naturalizer` (general and
scientific modes). Load alongside the domain registry.

**These are illustrative, NOT a closed vocabulary.** The list is
non-exhaustive by design: it shows the *kind* of connective tissue that
guides a reader, not a menu to pick from. A writer should invent the phrase
that fits the actual sentence and context — a pasted stock phrase is exactly
the mechanical AI move this registry exists to kill. Treat every entry below
as a model of *function*, not a usable phrase. When in doubt, prefer a phrase
that names the specific thing being transitioned to over a generic one.

## The job (what good reader-guidance does)

Mark pivots, orient the reader, invite attention, concede the reader's
objection, manage tension, and resume the thread — in passing, never as a
lecture. It lives in openings, signposts, and transitions, not in expanding
the body. The artifact stays focused.

**Grounding principle (sourced):** transitions are not decoration — "they are
words with particular meanings that tell the reader to think and react in a
particular way to your ideas" (UNC Writing Center). They make *existing*
organization visible; they never substitute for it: "Transitions cannot
substitute for good organization, but they can make your organization clearer"
(UNC). Good guidance is specific to the actual ideas being connected: "write a
transition that could only connect one specific paragraph to another specific
paragraph" (Purdue OWL), not one that "could connect any paragraph to any
other paragraph."

## The anti-pattern (what this is NOT)

These formulaic AI transitions are the *opposite* of reader-guidance — they
add connective tissue that carries no orientation, no voice, no pivot:

moreover / furthermore / additionally / consequently / thus / in today's era /
it's worth noting / it's important to understand / it's worth mentioning /
as previously mentioned / in conclusion / to summarize.

## Models by function (non-exhaustive)

Each row is a *family* of moves; the examples are ways it can be voiced.

| Function | What it does | Example voicings (invent better ones) |
|----------|--------------|----------------------------------------|
| Mark a pivot / groundwork done | Signals the setup is over, the pay-off begins | "With that in place, …" · "That settled, …" · "Now, the payoff: …" · "Now that we can X, we turn to Y." · "With the machinery in hand, …" |
| Orient / signpost what's next | Tells the reader where the argument goes | "Here is where it gets interesting." · "Two things follow from this." · "The upshot: …" · "Which brings us to …" · "Hold that thought." |
| Invite attention / explain why | Opens a claim so the reader follows the reasoning | "Notice that …" · "To see why, note that …" · "The reason is …" · "What this buys us is …" · "The intuition: …" |
| Concede / grant the reader's objection | Anticipates and grants a counterpoint before countering | "Granted, …" · "To be sure, …" · "It is true that … and yet …" · "Although this appears to be true, here's the real story." |
| Manage tension / surprise / counterpoint | Flags a catch, a turn, a reversal | "But here's the catch." · "And yet …" · "This cuts the other way." · "So far so good — except …" |
| Resume / return to the main line | Reconnects a detour to the thread | "Back to the main line." · "Which is why …" · "All of which is to say …" · "Where does that leave us?" |

## Rules for using them

- **Non-exhaustive.** Invent a phrase that fits the sentence. The list is a
  sketch of functions, not a fixed vocabulary.
- **Sparsity (sourced).** Guidance is the exception, not the norm. Most
  connections should be carried by idea order; a transition only highlights a
  relationship that already exists. Over-using them reads as over-explaining
  or patronising: "if you use too many transitions, your readers might feel
  like you are over-explaining connections that are already clear" (UW–Madison).
  Use the fewest transitions that keep the reader oriented.
- **One per boundary.** A single meaningful transition per paragraph or
  section turn. Stacked transitions ("Moreover, furthermore, additionally…")
  are the AI tell.
- **Name the thing.** Prefer "Now that we can invert the matrix, we…" over a
  generic "With that in place, we…" — the specific phrase is the human one.
- **Vary by function, not by word.** Alternate the *kind* of move (question,
  clause, conditional, declarative, concession) so the guidance doesn't become
  a repeated skeleton. Do NOT mechanically swap synonyms to avoid repetition —
  repeating a word that names the same concept is fine, and forced variation
  makes readers wonder if a second word means something different (Mensh &
  Kording). Vary the relationship, not the vocabulary.
- **Light touch.** These carry a reader from point to point; they never
  replace the focused content of the artifact.
- **Never in formal environments** (scientific mode): no rhetorical flourish
  inside `#definition`, `#theorem`, `#proof`, etc. Reader-guidance belongs in
  the surrounding expository prose only.

## Source notes

Functions and rules grounded in verified writing-guide sources: UNC Writing
Center ("Transitions", "Transitions (ESL)"); Purdue OWL ("Writing
Transitions"); UW–Madison Writing Center ("Using Transitional Words and
Phrases", "Connecting Ideas Through Transitions"); Mensh & Kording, *Ten
simple rules for structuring papers* (PLOS Comput Biol 2017); Grammarly
("How to Use Transition Sentences"). The "Concede / grant the reader's
objection" function and the "here's the catch" tension move are NOT present in
standard transition-word lists — they are function-based devices those lists
miss, which is precisely why they read human rather than mechanical. Honest
gaps: no authoritative *nonfiction* source was found for curiosity/suspense
devices (closest verified: UNC's "Although this idea appears to be true,
here's the real story" gloss and Jericho Writers' fiction-suspense mechanics);
the Duke scientific-writing metadiscourse handout could not be retrieved, so
no claim is attributed to it.
